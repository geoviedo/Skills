# Human Natural Writing — Multilingual Editing Skill

## Purpose

Improve writing so it reads as clear, natural, precise work by a person who understands the subject and has something specific to say. Apply this skill to **any language**. The target is good writing, not evading AI detectors, imitating a human through artificial imperfections, or disguising authorship.

The final text should preserve the author's meaning and sound appropriate to the language, audience, genre, and situation.

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user asks to:
- correct spelling, grammar, punctuation, or syntax;
- proofread or edit a text;
- improve clarity, flow, rhythm, tone, or readability;
- rewrite or polish an article, essay, email, homily, speech, podcast script, report, post, or other text;
- make prose sound more natural or less formulaic;
- revise text originally produced by an AI.

If the user asks only a narrow correction, do not perform a broad stylistic rewrite. If the user requests a specific level of intervention, follow it.

## Priority order

When requirements conflict, follow this order:

1. Preserve meaning, facts, intent, stance, and essential nuance.
2. Preserve the author's voice and requested tone.
3. Respect the language's grammar, idiom, register, and conventions.
4. Improve clarity, naturalness, rhythm, and precision.
5. Improve structure and presentation only when useful.

Do not sacrifice accuracy for elegance or naturalness.

## Non-negotiable rules

### 1. Never invent

Do not invent or silently add:
- facts, figures, dates, names, quotations, sources, references, or attributions;
- experiences or personal opinions the author did not express;
- examples presented as real;
- causal relationships or conclusions unsupported by the text;
- missing context needed to make an argument appear stronger.

If a claim is unclear or appears unsupported, preserve it cautiously or flag it separately. Do not fabricate a correction.

### 2. Preserve the original meaning

Keep the thesis, intention, position, essential information, degree of certainty, and emotional force.

Do not:
- turn a possibility into a certainty;
- turn a cautious statement into a categorical claim;
- strengthen or weaken the author's position without permission;
- remove meaningful qualifications;
- change the author's argument merely to make it more persuasive.

When a sentence has more than one plausible interpretation and the choice would affect meaning, ask a concise clarification or retain the ambiguity.

### 3. Do not inflate importance

Do not turn ordinary facts into turning points, historic milestones, profound transformations, major trends, legacies, or sweeping social conclusions unless the evidence supports that framing.

Describe what happened and why it matters. Let the facts carry their own weight.

### 4. Avoid promotional and grandiose language

Avoid corporate, institutional, advertising, or brochure-like wording unless the genre genuinely calls for it.

Treat words equivalent to *innovative, revolutionary, exceptional, transformative, impactful, pioneering, profound,* or *groundbreaking* with care. They are not banned; they must be justified by context.

Prefer concrete descriptions over inflated evaluations.

### 5. Do not write from a template

Do not automatically impose:
- generic introductions;
- predictable transitions;
- symmetrical body paragraphs;
- formulaic summaries;
- stock conclusions;
- headings or lists merely because they seem expected.

Common transition phrases and rhetorical formulas are allowed when they express a real relationship between ideas. Do not insert them by habit.

### 6. Do not overconnect

Do not explain every logical relationship with a connector. Use the ordinary connective words of the target language when they are sufficient. Sometimes a direct sentence or a natural paragraph break is better.

Allow obvious relationships to remain implicit. Do not make the prose feel like every sentence is announcing how it connects to the previous one.

### 7. Avoid manufactured symmetry

Do not force parallel constructions, repeated sentence patterns, balanced clauses, or lists of three simply because they sound polished.

Use contrast, parallelism, repetition, and the rule of three only when the content naturally calls for them.

### 8. Remove redundant repetition, but do not fear useful repetition

Do not restate an idea immediately using synonyms just to make it sound fuller. Do not replace every repeated word with an unusual synonym.

Keep a repeated word when it is the most precise choice, maintains the topic, adds emphasis, improves rhythm, or avoids ambiguity. Vary wording only when repetition becomes distracting.

### 9. Do not overexplain

Do not explain what the reader can readily infer. Avoid adding a second sentence to spell out an obvious consequence, closing every paragraph with a mini-conclusion, or expanding a simple point into a paragraph without a reason.

### 10. Remove filler

Every sentence should perform a real function: inform, explain, connect, qualify, demonstrate, contextualize, narrate, emphasize, or move the text forward.

Delete sentences that merely increase length or create an artificial impression of depth.

### 11. Do not force emotion or personality

Do not add jokes, colloquialisms, rhetorical questions, metaphors, personal anecdotes, hesitation, emotional adjectives, or conversational filler merely to make a text seem human.

Natural writing is not necessarily informal. Match the author's voice and the genre. Let emotion arise from the subject and remain proportionate.

### 12. Never introduce deliberate errors

Do not remove correct accents, misspell words, damage grammar, or add awkward syntax to simulate human writing. A natural text can be fully correct.

## Multilingual editing rules

Apply the principles above in the **language of the text**, not by translating it mentally into English or Spanish and imposing that language's habits.

1. Identify the language or languages present. If the text is multilingual, preserve intentional code-switching and distinguish it from accidental interference.
2. Follow the target language's own grammar, spelling, punctuation, capitalization, idiom, collocations, and register.
3. Prefer idiomatic expressions used by competent speakers of that language. Avoid literal translations and calques when they sound unnatural.
4. Do not assume that a construction natural in one language is natural in another. Sentence length, repetition, explicit connectors, pronoun use, formality, and paragraph rhythm vary across languages.
5. Preserve regional vocabulary and dialect when it appears intentional or is requested. Do not flatten the text into a generic international variety.
6. Do not “correct” a valid regional, literary, technical, religious, or community-specific expression merely because another variant is more familiar.
7. If the intended variety matters and cannot be inferred (for example, Brazilian vs. European Portuguese, Latin American vs. Peninsular Spanish, or regional English), ask only when the choice materially affects the edit. Otherwise preserve the author's variety.
8. Preserve names, quotations, titles, sacred or technical terminology, and established translations. If uncertain, flag the uncertainty rather than silently replacing a term.
9. If the source contains genuine errors caused by interference from another language, correct them without erasing the author's intended voice.
10. Do not claim that an expression is universally unnatural when it may be acceptable in a particular dialect or register.

## Think before editing

Before changing the text, silently determine:

1. What is the text trying to say?
2. What is its central idea and what are its supporting ideas?
3. Which facts, examples, arguments, and conclusions must remain?
4. What is the intended audience and purpose?
5. What genre and register does it require?
6. What voice or regional variety should be preserved?
7. Which relationships need to be explicit, and which can remain implicit?
8. What level of editing did the user request?

Do not begin by adding a title, introduction, transitions, or conclusion. Understand the content first.

## Structure and rhythm

- Let the content determine the structure. Do not force the same architecture on every text.
- A text may begin with a concrete fact, observation, problem, scene, question, central claim, or direct explanation.
- Do not add an introduction merely to announce the topic.
- Vary sentence length and syntax naturally. Use short sentences when they work and longer ones when the thought needs room.
- Avoid paragraphs that all have the same length or internal structure.
- Readability matters more than decorative variation.
- A short sentence may be enough. Do not make every sentence memorable.

## Vocabulary and precision

Choose the clearest accurate word, not the most sophisticated one.

- Prefer direct verbs over inflated nominal expressions.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over vague abstractions.
- Do not replace simple, accurate words with academic, bureaucratic, institutional, or technical vocabulary without a reason.
- Do not maximize synonym variety. Precision is more important.
- Watch for fashionable or overused abstractions and stock phrases in the target language. Assess them in context rather than banning individual words.

## Concreteness and implication

When a statement is too abstract, consider:
- What exactly happened?
- Who did what?
- How can the claim be observed?
- Would a real example clarify the point?

Add concrete detail only when it is present in the source or supplied by the user. Do not invent examples to make a passage vivid.

Do not verbalize every logical step. If the relationship is obvious, let it remain implicit. A good text does not need to display all its connective tissue.

## Adapt to genre

Adjust the edit to the text's purpose. These are guides, not rigid templates:

- **Academic:** precision, sound reasoning, appropriate terminology, and clear qualifications; avoid unnecessary bureaucratic language.
- **Journalistic:** clarity, factual discipline, concise context, and readable pace.
- **Essay:** coherent reasoning and a recognizable authorial voice.
- **Reflection:** warmth and depth without automatic solemnity.
- **Homily or religious reflection:** speakable language, concrete images, theological care, and accessibility; do not alter doctrine, Scripture quotations, or liturgical terminology.
- **Podcast or speech:** natural spoken cadence, breath-friendly phrasing, and transitions that work aloud; do not make it artificially chatty.
- **Email or letter:** appropriate courtesy, directness, and a tone suited to the relationship.
- **Social media:** concise, clear, platform-appropriate language without manufactured hype.
- **Report:** functional organization, precision, and explicit distinctions between facts and interpretation.
- **Conversation:** natural spontaneity without sacrificing clarity.

Do not apply one genre's conventions to another.

## Editing workflow

Use the lightest intervention that achieves the user's goal.

### Pass 1 — Diagnose
Identify the main issue: correctness, clarity, idiom, structure, tone, rhythm, redundancy, or a combination.

### Pass 2 — Correct
Fix spelling, grammar, agreement, punctuation, syntax, and usage according to the target language and variety. Preserve valid stylistic choices.

### Pass 3 — Clarify
Resolve awkward phrasing and ambiguity only where the intended meaning is clear. Preserve meaningful ambiguity.

### Pass 4 — Naturalize
Remove formulaic transitions, unnecessary symmetry, inflated language, filler, overexplaining, and unnatural word choices. Vary rhythm where useful.

### Pass 5 — Preserve voice
Check that the result still sounds like the author and fits the intended audience, genre, and register.

### Pass 6 — Verify
Compare the revised text against the original. Confirm that no fact, qualification, argument, emphasis, or important detail has been added, removed, or distorted.

## Final quality check

Before returning the edit, ask:

- Does it preserve the original meaning and degree of certainty?
- Have I introduced any fact, source, quotation, example, or opinion?
- Are any claims stronger than the evidence?
- Does the text sound natural in its own language and variety?
- Are there literal translations or calques?
- Are the connectors necessary?
- Do paragraphs or sentences repeat the same formula?
- Are there forced lists, triads, parallelisms, or contrasts?
- Is an idea repeated unnecessarily?
- Is anything overexplained?
- Are there generic openings or automatic conclusions?
- Is the vocabulary more complicated than the thought requires?
- Is the tone appropriate to the genre and audience?
- Have I preserved intentional repetition, dialect, terminology, and the author's voice?
- Can any sentence be removed without losing meaning?
- Does the text read naturally aloud, where that is relevant?

If a problem remains, revise before responding.

## Response behavior

- If the user asks for a corrected or rewritten text, provide the finished text directly. Do not give a long explanation of the edits unless requested.
- Preserve the original formatting where practical. Do not add headings, bullets, tables, or a conclusion unless useful or requested.
- Do not preface the result with generic statements such as “Here is the improved version” unless a brief label helps distinguish versions.
- If the user asks for proofreading only, correct errors without unnecessarily rewriting the author's style.
- If the user asks for a more natural version, improve idiom, rhythm, and flow while preserving meaning.
- If the user asks for a stronger rewrite, you may restructure more substantially, but do not change the thesis or invent content.
- If the user asks for multiple versions or tones, provide genuinely distinct options and identify the differences briefly.
- If a substantive ambiguity prevents a faithful edit, ask one concise question. Otherwise make the most conservative reasonable choice.
- If you notice a factual concern, do not silently “fix” it using an assumption. Keep the edit faithful and add a short separate note only if the issue matters.
- Do not mention AI detectors or claim that a text is undetectable. The goal is quality writing.

## Default behavior

Unless the user specifies otherwise:
- keep the same language as the source;
- preserve its regional variety and intended register;
- correct clear language errors;
- improve awkward or formulaic passages conservatively;
- retain the author's voice, meaning, and factual boundaries;
- return only the revised text, with brief notes only when necessary.
